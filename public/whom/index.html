Whom it forgives: the register on one axis

Every worked entry ends in a box called “Whom it forgives”. This form collects them. Three of the rules have a nominal value to be wrong from, so they can be drawn on one scale, in percent of that value: to the right, error that pays whoever controls it; to the left, error that costs them. Set one fault and run it through all three.

2Finding

The edges of a window do not say whom it forgives. Drawn here, the jar rule and the meter rule have the same shape, tight on the side that pays the one in control and wide on the side that costs them. The jar rule forgives the packer; the meter rule forgives the passenger.

The difference is in how each is measured. The meter is judged on its own error, against fixed edges. The jar lot is judged on a sample’s average, against a margin that grows with the sample’s spread. So a steady packer half a percent short is caught nearly every time, and a careless one passes about 2 times in 3 (box 3, the first two buttons).

4Why the jar rule flips

At a spread of 0.25% (1.1 g), a lot of jars half a percent short (2.3 g) passes 0 times in 1,000: the average is short by far more than 0.635 times a 1.1-gram standard deviation. At a spread of 1% (4.5 g), the margin is about 2.9 g and the same shortfall passes about 650 times in 1,000. The coin and the meter rules only get kinder to the aim as the spread falls. The jar rule gets harsher.

So the jar rule’s own window, 4.38% wide on the packer’s side, is not what a steady packer can use. What it can use is the dark band, and the dark band is made of its own scatter (Entry 001).

5The one rule that minds the other side

Press “Two percent against them”. Jars 2% over pass: Handbook 133 “does not specify limits of overfilling”. A meter 2% slow passes: its slow edge is 4%. Coins 2% heavy fail every time.

The coin remedy is the only one of the three with edges an equal distance either side. Heavy coins cost the Mint metal, so the remedy protects the Mint from itself about as much as it protects anyone from the Mint. Its lean is in the measuring: an average of 88 coins scatters about a ninth as much as one coin, and the remedy does not shrink with it (Entry 003).

7The position, tested against the register

The site’s position has two halves. “A tolerance is a decision about whose mistakes to forgive”: four entries in, this holds, with one strain. The chocolate rule forgives a distribution of filth, not a person, and which lots it forgives turns over at its own limit. A rule can forgive a shape that no one chose.

“Read one closely and you can tell who wrote it”: this half has not been tested. Each entry found whom its rule forgives. None has yet found who argued for the edge, or when. Until one does, the register shows what the rules do. It has not shown who wanted them to do it.

8Not modeled

One percent means a different thing in each rule, and the axis lays them side by side without making them equal. The jar edge is a per-package limit for a 454 g label (other labels have other limits), checked with the average on a sample of 12 jars from a lot of 12 to 250. The coin remedy is for the ten pence as enacted in 1971. The meter edges leave out the extra 100 ft allowed when the test includes the first fare interval. In box 3, fills, coin weights and cab errors are normal around the aim, and the jar margin in the dark band takes the sample’s standard deviation to equal the line’s.
