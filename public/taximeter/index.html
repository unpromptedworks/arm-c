The lopsided meter: a taximeter may read 1% long or 4% short

NIST Handbook 44, the code US weights-and-measures inspectors test taximeters against, lets a meter register up to 1% more distance than the cab drove, or up to 4% less. You set the meters for a fleet of cabs: choose their aim and their scatter, and drive them over the inspector’s measured mile.

2Finding

This window leans toward the passenger, and the scatter between meters is charged to the cab. If a fleet’s meters scatter 1% from cab to cab, the fleet must set them at least 1.3% slow for 99 in 100 to pass. Its passengers then pay for at least 1.3% less distance than they ride, on average. Centre a window of the same width on true distance, ±3.45%, and the same fleet could set its meters 1.1% fast.

Scatter is the standard deviation of the meters’ errors around the fleet’s aim. It covers whatever makes one cab’s meter differ from another’s on test day: calibration, tires, wear. Most of the window lies on the short side, from −5.9% to +1% on the test mile, so the fast edge is the one a fleet runs into. Box 7 has every scatter; box 5 offers the centred window.

3The allowance

“T.1.1. On Distance Tests. – Maintenance and acceptance tolerances for taximeters shall be as follows: (a) On Overregistration: 1 % of the interval under test. (b) On Underregistration: 4 % of the interval under test, with an added tolerance of 30 m or 100 ft whenever the initial interval is included in the interval under test.”

NIST Handbook 44 (2026), § 5.54, T.1.1. The same numbers serve as acceptance tolerances (for a new or repaired meter) and maintenance tolerances (for one in service).

4The test

“The distance test of a taximeter … shall include at least duplicate runs of sufficient length to cover at least the third money drop or 1 mi, whichever is greater.” On a road test the cab is driven “over a precisely measured road course”, carrying two persons, and its tires are then checked against the pressure posted in the vehicle.

Same section, N.1.1(a), N.1.2, N.1.3.1.1 and N.1.3.1.2. A money drop is one step of the fare. This page tests one mile, the shortest run the code allows when the first fare steps come sooner.

7Where a fleet can aim

The fastest aim that passes 99 cabs in 100, by scatter

The solid curve is the fastest aim at which 99 meters in 100 pass the window you chose; the dashed curve is the other window. Above a curve, more than 1 cab in 100 fails. Below the dotted zero line, the fleet’s meters run slow on average and the passenger gets the difference. The ring is your fleet. As written, the curve crosses zero at a scatter of 0.43%: any fleet looser than that must aim slow. It ends near 1.34%, past which no aim gets 99 in 100 through. Every point is computed from the normal distribution (a polynomial approximation, good to about one part in ten million), not simulated.

8The clock, too

The time test is the initial interval, four later intervals, and an average of at least four consecutive later intervals (N.2). On that average, the slow side is fifteen times the fast.

9Whom it forgives

The meter rides in the cab, whose tires the code makes the user keep at the posted pressure (UR.1). The passenger has no way to measure the ride. Handbook 44 allows four times as much distance error against the cab as against the passenger. The cab cannot choose which way its errors fall, so the meters’ ordinary scatter becomes a price cut: a fleet that wants its cabs to pass has to aim short of true.

In the three entries before this one, the allowance forgave the maker’s error: the packer’s spread, the processor’s contamination, the Mint’s aim. This one forgives the device owner only when the owner is the one who loses. It is still a decision about whose mistakes to forgive; this one sides with the passenger.

10Not modeled

Time charges and the interference test (T.1.3); the fifth-wheel test’s extra allowance of twice the transfer standard’s standard deviation (T.2); meters that take distance from GPS or a network rather than the wheels, which the code has covered since 2017 (N.1.2.1); and the fare steps themselves, which make a meter’s reading jump in drops rather than run smoothly. The page draws each meter’s error from a normal distribution around your aim with your scatter, and treats the duplicate runs as one, since a meter with a fixed error reads the same on both. It says nothing about how much real meters scatter, or where any fleet sets them; the 1% in box 2 is an example, not a measurement. One report points the same way: when state inspectors road-tested Raleigh’s taxis on a measured mile in 2006, a local news story reported that “so far, inspectors say they have found more meters undercharging than overcharging.” It gives no figures.2 Handbook 44 is a model code; NIST says “all 50 States adopt either the current version or an older edition” of it, and local rules may add to it.3
