The fast lean: a chronometer may gain 6 seconds a day and lose 4

ISO 3159 is the test a Swiss watch movement passes to be sold as a “chronometer”. Its first criterion lets the movement’s mean rate run from 4 seconds a day slow to 6 fast. You regulate a movement and send it through the fifteen days.

2Finding

Every official window for a Swiss mechanical chronometer’s rate leans fast, and the one on the certificate is not the one on the wrist.

The bureaux’ window before 1973, −1 to +10 seconds a day, was centred at +4.5; ISO 3159’s −4 to +6 is centred at +1; the METAS Master Chronometer’s 0 to +5 at +2.5. None is centred on true time. The quartz chronometer, which COSC tests to its own limits, is the exception: ±0.07 s/d at 23 °C, centred on zero. And ISO 3159 measures the rate at 23 °C, while allowing it to change by up to 0.6 s/d for each degree. At the 33 °C that METAS uses for a watch on a wrist, a movement that passes every criterion may run from −10 to +12 s/d, if its rate changes evenly with temperature (box 6).

3The allowance

Category 1 (casing diameter over 20 mm), in seconds a day: mean daily rate −4 +6; mean variation in rates 2; greatest variation in rates 5; difference between the rates in horizontal and vertical positions −6 +8; greatest deviation in rates 10; variation in rate as a function of temperature ±0.6 s/(d·°C); resumption of the rate ±5. “The minimum requirements are considered to be absolute limits and no calculation result shall be rounded off.”

ISO 3159:2009, clause 7. The 1976 edition gives the same mean rate, −4 +6. Category 2, smaller watches, is allowed −5 +8.

4The test

Fifteen days, the watch wound each day and read against reference time to ±0.5 s, “rounded to the nearest full second”. Days 1–10 at 23 °C, two days in each of five positions; day 11 at 8 °C, day 12 at 23, day 13 at 38, all dial up; days 14 and 15 back at 23 °C, 6 o’clock up. The mean rate is the mean of the first ten days only.

Clauses 5 and 6; the daily winding, “at precisely the same time each day”, is from Wikipedia’s COSC article (source 2). Positions are named as in ISO 3158: 6H, 3H and 9H are 6, 3 and 9 o’clock up; FH is dial down (fond, caseback, up); CH is dial up (cadran haut).

8Whom it forgives

The window forgives the maker an error in regulation, and forgives it more on the fast side: up to 6 seconds a day there, 4 on the slow. A movement regulated a little fast has a margin of 5 seconds either way; one regulated true has 4. The wearer pays for that lean in minutes early rather than minutes late: at +6 s/d a watch set right on the first of the month is three minutes fast by the thirtieth; at −4 it is two minutes slow.

The second forgiving is quieter. The test is the maker’s conditions: a movement wound every day, at 23 °C, turned through five positions on a schedule. Temperature is held to a limit of its own, but between the lab’s 23 °C and a wrist’s 33 that limit allows a certified rate to move by 6 s/d either way: more than the window’s whole slow side, and as much as its fast side. METAS, which tests at 33 °C and allows no losing at all, is the first of the three to write the wrist into the rule.

9Who wrote it

This page cannot say why the lean is fast. What the record here shows is that it has moved. Before 1973 the bureaux allowed a mean of −1 to +10 in five positions, a window 11 seconds wide whose centre lay 4.5 seconds fast. The rule of 1973, in ISO 3159 since its 1976 edition, narrowed it by one second and moved its centre to +1. METAS moved it back toward fast, to +2.5. A watch that ran 3 seconds slow would have been refused in 1972, certified from 1973, and refused again by METAS.

The 1961–73 figure comes from Wikipedia’s article on COSC, which cites F. von Osterhausen, Wristwatch Chronometers (1997); the book itself was not read for this page.
