Insects in chocolate: the test reads the shape of the mess

The FDA does not act against chocolate for containing insect fragments. It acts when six 100-gram subsamples show too many. You make the lot. Set how many fragments it holds and how evenly they are spread through it, then hand it to the analyst.

2Finding

Below the limit, the test is harder on patchy chocolate than on dirty chocolate. A lot with 55 fragments in every 100 g, spread evenly, draws action about 1 time in 20; a lot averaging 40, but in patches, about 1 time in 4. Above the limit it turns around: a lot averaging 70, spread evenly, is caught almost every time, and the same lot in heavy patches escapes about 1 time in 6.

Patchiness is how far the true density of one 100-gram piece typically sits from the lot average. At 60%, a piece from a lot averaging 40 typically holds somewhere from about 16 to 64. At 150%, most pieces are nearly clean and a few are very dirty. “Heavy patches” above means 150%. The figures come from simulating the test below; box 7 shows the rest of the range.

3Test one: the average

The analyst examines six 100-gram subsamples. If they contain 60 or more insect fragments per 100 g on average, that is, 360 or more in all, the chocolate is subject to action.

FDA Compliance Policy Guide Sec. 515.700, criterion 1a. Fragments are counted by AOAC method 965.38.

4Test two: any one piece

If any one subsample contains 90 or more fragments, the chocolate is subject to action “even if the overall average of all the subsamples is less than 60.”

Criterion 1b. Six pieces get six chances to cross it. A lot that is the same throughout almost never does: at 55 per 100 g, a count of 90 is nearly five standard deviations of chance away.

7Map of actioned lots

Chance a lot draws action, by average and patchiness

Each cell is 300 simulated examinations of six subsamples, run in your browser when the page loads. The stronger the red, the likelier the action. The ring is your lot. Along the bottom row, the evenly mixed lots, action arrives only near 60, and steeply. Higher up, it arrives early and partly, from criterion 1b, long before the average is anywhere near the limit. Right of the limit the order reverses: the even lots are caught every time, and the patchy ones sometimes escape, because all six pieces can come from the cleaner parts of the lot.

8Whom it forgives

The second test is there for a reason: a lot can be clean on average and still hold a piece that is not, and an average alone would never find it. But six pieces are a small look at a large lot, and the result is a rule that reads the shape of the contamination as well as its amount.

Below the limit it forgives the even lot: fragments spread thinly through every bar pass up to 60, while fragments that sit together are caught well below it. Above the limit it forgives the patchy lot, which can hide its dirty parts from six pieces; an even lot over the limit has nowhere to hide. Mixing the lot more thoroughly helps a maker who is under the limit and hurts one who is over it. The FDA closes one route: food at or above an action level may not be blended with another lot to bring it down, and doing so “renders the final food unlawful regardless of the defect level of the finished food.”

9Note on rodent hairs

The same guide sets limits for rodent hairs, and the FDA’s two documents word them differently. The Compliance Policy Guide acts on an average of more than 1.0 hair per 100 g, or any subsample with more than 3. The Food Defect Levels Handbook says “1 or more” and “3 or more.”

Six hairs across the six subsamples, an average of exactly 1.0, is actionable under the handbook and not under the guide. So is a single subsample with exactly 3. The insect limits, 60 and 90, read “or more” in both.

10Not modeled

How the six subsamples are drawn from a shipment; the recovery rate of the counting method; rodent hairs and cocoa shell, which have their own limits; and the FDA’s statement that poor manufacturing practice may draw action “without regard to the action level.” The simulation treats each 100-gram subsample as drawn from its own patch of the lot, whose true density is spread around the lot average with the patchiness you set (a gamma distribution), and counts fragments in it as a Poisson count. This is the usual model for clumped counts, not one taken from FDA data, which this page does not have.
