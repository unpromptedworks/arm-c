Entry 005 · Two tests for one tablet · USP <905>
1Item

Two tests for one tablet: the count that became a number

A tablet labeled 10 mg does not hold exactly 10 mg, and the United States Pharmacopeia says how far a batch’s tablets may wander. In 1995 its test counted tablets: assay ten, and every one must lie between 85 and 115 percent of the label. The test that replaced it, harmonized with the European and Japanese pharmacopoeias, counts nothing at first. It combines the ten tablets’ average and their spread into one “acceptance value” and passes the batch if that value is 15.0 or less. You run the tablet press. Set its aim and its scatter, and put the same thirty tablets through both tests.

2Finding

The old test held the stray tablets to the same share whatever the aim. The new one lets out more the further the aim drifts. At the widest scatter that still passes 99 batches in 100, about 7 tablets in 1,000 fall outside 85–115% under the 1995 test, at any aim. Under the harmonized test it is 9 in 1,000 for a batch aimed at the label, 18 for one aimed 10% low, and 34 for one aimed 12% low.

Scatter is the standard deviation of single tablets around the press’s aim, in percent of the label claim. The figures come from simulating 20,000 batches at each aim (box 8). A share outside 85–115% is the chance that a patient’s tablet is more than 15% off the label. Near the label the two tests pass almost the same presses. They part as the aim moves away, because the old test asks about tablets and the new one asks about the sample’s statistics.

3The 1995 test

For compressed tablets, USP 23: assay 10 tablets. The batch passes if each lies “within the range of 85.0% to 115.0% of the label claim” and their relative standard deviation is 6.0% or less.

If one tablet is outside 85–115% but none outside 75–125%, or the RSD is over 6.0%, assay 20 more. The batch then passes if no more than 1 of the 30 is outside 85–115%, none is outside 75–125%, and the RSD of the 30 “does not exceed 7.8%.” Two strays in the first ten fail the batch at once.

USP 23 (1995), <905> Uniformity of Dosage Units, Criteria (A), as transcribed by Utrecht University (source 1). Short quotations.

4The harmonized test

Assay 10 tablets. With X̄ their mean and s their standard deviation, in percent of label:

AV = |M − X̄| + 2.4·s

where M is X̄ itself if X̄ lies between 98.5 and 101.5, and otherwise the nearer of those two. The batch passes if AV ≤ 15.0. If not, assay 20 more and recompute on all 30 with 2.0 in place of 2.4; the batch passes if that AV is 15.0 or less and no tablet is more than 25% from M.

USP <905>, harmonized text official 1 December 2011, Table 2 and Criteria, for a target content of 100% (source 2).

At every aim the harmonized test allows a little more scatter, between 0.2 and 0.5 points. Near the label that buys little: at 100%, the extra 0.26 points lets out 3 more tablets in 1,000. Away from the label it buys more, because the allowance is spent on the side where the tablets are already close to 85%. The 1995 test cannot be spent that way: it counts the tablets past 85%, so whatever the aim, a press that passes 99 times in 100 puts about the same share there.

9Whom it forgives

Both tests forgive the press its scatter, and both charge for aiming away from the label. They differ in what they charge with. The 1995 test spent its tolerance on tablets: it forgave one stray in thirty and no more, so the cost of a poor aim was paid in strays. The acceptance value spends it on the maker’s statistics. The first 1.5% of aim is free, each further point of aim costs 1.0 of the 15.0, and each point of standard deviation costs 2.4. The test prices the aim and the scatter, not the tablets the patient takes, so where the aim and the scatter are cheap, the tablets past 85% go unpriced.

So the rewrite moved the forgiving. The old count forgave the maker its aim and held the patient’s share of stray tablets fixed; the acceptance value forgives a little more scatter everywhere and lets the patient’s share grow where the aim is poor. A batch aimed 10% low is not what anyone sets out to make, and another test (box 10) may stand in its way.

10The other test

Content uniformity is not the only check on a batch. Each tablet monograph has its own Assay, a limit on the batch’s average content; for levothyroxine sodium tablets, before a revision USP announced in 2008, it was 90.0 to 110.0 percent of the label. Where the Assay is 90.0–110.0%, a batch aimed 12% low fails before uniformity matters. A batch aimed 10% low sits on the Assay’s edge, and there the harmonized test lets out 1.8% of tablets past 85% to the old test’s 0.7%.

The Assay is set drug by drug, and it can tighten. In 2008 USP announced it would narrow levothyroxine sodium tablets from 90.0–110.0% to “not less than 95.0% and not more than 105.0%” of the labelled amount. Within 95–105%, the two uniformity tests differ by about 3 tablets in 1,000 or fewer (box 8).

11Not modeled

Weight variation, which the harmonized chapter allows for some tablets in place of assaying each one; capsules, which under USP 23 were allowed three strays in thirty; a target content above 101.5% (case 2 of the reference value); monographs that set other limits; the error of the assay itself; and how the 1995 rule changes when a monograph’s potency limits average above 100%. The simulation draws each tablet’s content from a normal distribution around your aim with your scatter, independently. Real batches drift, blend unevenly and have heavier tails. The 99-in-100 edge is a choice; at a laxer edge (passing half the time) the harmonized test is the stricter of the two away from the label: aimed at 95% with 6% scatter, it passes 1 batch in 2, the 1995 test 2 in 3.
