On the line: who gets the benefit of the doubt?

Each worked entry on this site is a rule that draws a line and an inspection that checks a sample against it. Here the three inspections face the same test: a lot that sits exactly on its rule’s line, and then lots a little past it. How often does each one pass?

2Finding

On the line, the three inspections split the doubt three ways. A jar line that averages exactly 454 g passes 39 times in 40, however steady it is. A coin press aimed exactly at the edge of the remedy passes 1 time in 2. A chocolate lot at exactly 60 fragments per 100 g escapes 1 time in 2 if the fragments are spread evenly, and only 1 time in 4 or 5 if they come in patches.

The US packer gets the benefit of the doubt, by design. The Mint gets none from its inspection: what the coin rule forgives, it forgives in the wording of the law (box 7). The chocolate maker gets less of it once the lot is patchy.

6Why the jar line passes 39 times in 40

The handbook lets the sample average fall short by 0.635 times the sample’s standard deviation. For a lot whose true average is exactly 454 g, the sample’s shortfall divided by its standard error follows Student’s t with 11 degrees of freedom, and 0.635 × √12 = 2.20 is that distribution’s 97.5th percentile. So the lot passes the average test 97.5% of the time at any spread. Only when the spread is wide enough for single jars to fall 19.9 g short (about 7 g and up) does the rate drop.

The handbook says the margin gives the packer “an approximately 95 % level of confidence”; as Entry 001 notes, the table prints the 97.5th-percentile factor. Either way it is a deliberate gift to the packer, sized to the sample.

7Why the others get a coin toss

Coins. The Act gives the jury a line and no margin: the kilogram is weighed and its average is inside 64.6 mg or not. A press aimed exactly at the edge lands either side of it equally often. The forgiving in this rule happens earlier, in its wording: by measuring a per-coin remedy on an average of 88, it lets the aim drift almost the whole remedy before the line is reached (Entry 003).

Chocolate. The action level is a plain average with no margin either way, so an even lot at 60 is caught half the time. The second clause, 90 in any one subsample, is what moves it: the patchier the lot, the likelier one subsample crosses 90, and the more often a lot on the line is caught. Further past the line, patchiness turns the other way and lets lots escape (Entry 002).

8What this does to the site’s position

The register’s position is that a tolerance is a decision about whose mistakes to forgive. Side by side, the three rules show that the decision can sit in different places. In the jar rule it sits in the inspection, which leans to the packer in plain sight. In the coin rule the inspection is even-handed and the lean is in the definition. In the chocolate rule neither leans by design; the shape of the contamination decides. Reading only the inspection would have got the coin rule wrong.

9Not modeled

The models are those of the entry pages, with the same assumptions: normal fills and coin weights, gamma-distributed local insect density with Poisson counts. The jar curve is for a lot of 12 to 250 packages (sample of 12); the coin curve is for the light side, and the heavy side is its mirror. The chocolate line is an action level, a point at which the FDA may act, not a guarantee that it will. Curves are forced to fall as the lot moves past the line, which removes simulation noise and nothing else.
