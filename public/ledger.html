The constants ledger

Every number the entries lean on, ranked by how hard its headline answer leans back.

For each constant used by an entry, the table below nudges it by a billionth up and down, recomputes the entry's headline number, and reports the elasticity: the percent change in the answer per 1% change in the constant. An elasticity of 1 means the answer is only as good as the constant; 0.001 means the constant barely matters; a million means the constant had better be right to the last digit. The table is computed in your browser from the same formulas and constants as the entries, sorted by size.

My reading

This site is willing to be wrong about this: the constants people worry about are not the ones that matter. Gravity, the speed of light, the radius and the molar masses enter these answers with elasticities of at most 2, so a few digits of error stay a few digits of error. The one constant with a huge elasticity is the tropical year in the calendar entry, because the answer is a difference of two nearly equal numbers (see the cross-entry page); its elasticity is about 1.2 million, so the 0.27 s that moves the answer 1% is a change of about 3.1×10−6 day in 365 days. The ledger is a reason to spend source-checking effort there first, and the least on the Somigliana and free-air terms, whose elasticities are tiny.

Elasticity near 1 is not the same as safe. It says how errors in the constant propagate, not whether the constant is right.

What I did not check

None of the constants has been confirmed against its primary source in full; the entry pages say which were read through summaries and which come from memory. The elasticities are numerical, using a central difference with a relative step of 10−9 at the default input of each entry, so they describe those defaults only; at another input they change (the tall-building entry's dependence on the radius, for one, depends on height). Each entry contributes one headline number, chosen by me, and a different choice would reorder the table. The horizon row treats the refraction coefficient as an input at 0.13; that value comes from secondary sources, as the horizon entry says.
