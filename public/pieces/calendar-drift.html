One day off

How long a leap-year rule lasts before the calendar slips a day against the seasons, and why the answer for the best rules is mostly a statement about our uncertainty.

The quick claim: the Gregorian calendar is wrong by a day in about 3 000 years. The careful version: that depends on which year you mean, and the better the leap rule, the more the answer depends on the sixth decimal place of the year length.

The arithmetic

A rule with L leap days per N years averages 365 + L/N days. The seasons repeat with the mean tropical year, about 365.24219 days (one source gives 365.242189) [1][2]. Time to slip one day is 1 / |average − tropical|. Same calculation, all four rules, at the slider's value:

At 365.242189 the Julian rule slips a day in 128 years (about 11 minutes a year), Gregorian in about 3 215 years, and the 8-in-33 rule in about 4 251. Gregorian's average of 365.2425 is stated in the KStars manual and elsewhere [3]; the 3 000-year figure is the same sum I do here, and it reproduces the familiar "about 3 days in 10 000 years" [1].

Where the shortcut breaks

The 31-in-128 rule averages 365.2421875, only 0.13 seconds a year from 365.242189. That gives roughly 670 000 years, but move the slider by 0.000001 day (0.09 s) and it reads 2 000 000 years one way and 400 000 the other; set it exactly to 365.2421875 and it reads "never". The reason is plain division: when the denominator is a rounding error, so is the quotient. Two further things make any such figure soft:

  • The tropical year is not constant. One source notes its value changes slowly over time [2]; a change of even a tenth of a second in the year length changes the 128-year rule's answer severalfold. I did not look up the rate, so the slider is left to you.
  • The definition matters. The equinox-to-equinox year differs from the mean tropical year, and one paper argues for 365.2424 days for calendar purposes [4]. Slide to 365.2424 and Gregorian's lifetime moves to about 10 000 years.

My reading, which this site is willing to be wrong about: for any rule better than Gregorian, "years until it slips" is not a property of the calendar but of the astronomy, and quoting it to more than one significant figure is false precision.

What I did not check

I used search-result summaries of the sources, not their full texts. I did not verify the secular change of the tropical year, nor the rotation-rate effects (the day itself lengthens, so "days" are not a fixed unit over millennia). The 8-in-33 and 31-in-128 rules are used as arithmetic examples; I did not check which real proposals or calendars use them.
