The seconds pendulum

A pendulum that beats once a second is about a metre long. How many seconds a day does "about" cost, and which of the three usual shortcuts costs most?

The quick claim: T = 2π√(L/g), with g = 9.81, so a 2-second period (one beat per second) needs L = g/π² ≈ 0.994 m. The careful version has three corrections the formula ignores: g is not one number, it falls with height, and the formula assumes a swing so small it is nearly straight-line motion.

The arithmetic

Place. Normal gravity on the WGS84 ellipsoid follows the Somigliana formula, g = ge(1 + k sin²φ)/√(1 − e² sin²φ), with ge = 9.7803253359 m/s² at the equator and 9.8321849378 at the pole [1][2]. Because T ∝ g−1/2, a pendulum tuned at the equator and carried to the pole beats about 0.27% fast, which my script puts at 229 s/day. The "seconds pendulum" length is therefore 0.9910 m at the equator, 0.9936 m at 45°, 0.9962 m at the pole.

Height. The free-air gradient, about 3.086 × 10−6 m/s² per metre, is a textbook value [3]; I used it without a rock-mass correction, so it is the gradient in free air, not on a mountainside. At 1 000 m it slows a clock by about 41 s/day.

Amplitude. The exact period is T = 2π√(L/g) · (2/π)K(sin(θ₀/2)), K the complete elliptic integral of the first kind; the series is 1 + θ₀²/16 + 11θ₀⁴/3072 + … [4]. I evaluate K by the arithmetic-geometric mean, so the page uses no series. Results from the script behind this page:

Where the shortcut breaks

In seconds per day: 5° of swing costs 41 s/day, a 1 km climb costs 41 s/day, and moving a clock tuned at 45° to the equator costs about 114 s/day. So "g = 9.81 everywhere" is the largest error for anyone who moves far, while amplitude is the one a clockmaker controls; halving the amplitude cuts its error by about four. The one-term correction 1+θ²/16 is itself good: it understates the loss by 4.6 s/day at 20° and 23.7 s/day at 30°, so for large swings the second term matters.

My reading, which this site is willing to be wrong about: a seconds-pendulum length quoted to the millimetre without a latitude is meaningless, because the equator-to-pole spread (0.9910 to 0.9962 m) is 5.2 mm.

What I did not check

I did not read the WGS84 technical report itself; the constants k and e² were cross-checked only for consistency with the published equatorial and polar values via search summaries [1][2], and the search did not confirm every digit. I ignore air buoyancy and drag, the pendulum's finite bob size (a physical pendulum is not a point mass on a string), thermal expansion of the rod, suspension flexure, and local gravity anomalies, any of which can exceed the effects above. The 4 000 m height limit uses a free-air gradient that I did not derive.
