One degree per 300 metres

The camper's rule that water boils 1 °C lower for every 300 m of height is within 0.5 °C of the steam tables up to 6 km. The weather on the day moves the answer by more than the rule's error does.

The quick claim: water boils at 100 − h/300 °C at height h metres. Two things stand behind it: how fast air pressure falls with height, and how fast the boiling point falls with pressure. The rule hides both inside one number. This entry computes both, from the international steam-table equation and the standard-atmosphere formula, and asks which one the rule is more likely to get wrong.

The arithmetic

Boiling point from pressure. Water boils when its saturation pressure equals the surrounding pressure. I used the saturation-pressure equation of IAPWS-IF97 (Eqs. 29 to 30, coefficients from its Table 34) [1], which the release states is valid from 273.15 K to the critical point, and inverted it by bisection. I checked my code against the release's own test values in Table 35: 300 K gives 0.003 536 589 MPa, 500 K gives 2.638 898 MPa and 600 K gives 12.344 315 MPa, which match the printed 0.353 658 941 × 10⁻², 0.263 889 776 × 10¹ and 0.123 443 146 × 10² to every digit printed. Near sea level the boiling point changes by 0.0267 °C per hPa; at 2 000 m, 0.0338; at 5 000 m, 0.0467. Steam gets less sensitive to pressure the more pressure there is, which is why the fall per metre is not constant.

Pressure from height. The troposphere form of the 1976 US Standard Atmosphere, p = 101 325 · (1 − 2.25577×10⁻⁵ h)^5.25588 Pa [2], where the exponent and sea-level values are as that standard states them. This is a model of an average day: it gives 79 495 Pa at 2 000 m and 54 020 Pa at 5 000 m.

The comparison. On the standard day, water boils at 99.97 °C at sea level (the IF97 value at 101 325 Pa; I did not compare it against another source). The drop from there against the rule:

The two effects partly cancel: pressure falls more slowly per metre as you climb, while the boiling point falls more quickly per hPa. The rule is the point where the two curves cross, and it is a good fit.

Where the shortcut breaks

As a formula, the rule reaches 1% error in the drop at about 8 800 m, roughly the height of Everest, which is a bound by my arithmetic rather than a measured one. That is a rule you can stop worrying about.

The assumption is the trouble. The rule, and my table, take the standard day. Real sea-level pressure wanders by tens of hPa. Thirty hPa below standard (983 hPa) moves the boiling point down by about 0.8 °C at sea level and more with height; that is larger than the rule's error at every height in the table below 8 000 m. So a quoted "water boils at 93.3 °C at 2 000 m" has an uncertainty from the barometer, not from the formula, of about ±1 °C. Slide the second control to see it.

My reading, which this site is willing to be wrong about: for cooking at altitude the rule is good enough and the weather correction is not worth making, because the food does not respond to a degree; for calibrating a thermometer against boiling water, it is the reverse, and you need the day's station pressure and not a height. I have not checked either against a cooking or metrology reference.

What I did not check

I did not compare IF97's 99.97 °C against another source, such as the ITS-90 definition of the boiling point. I did not check the 1976 standard directly: the constants and exponent are from a secondary description of it [2], and I confirmed the 2 000 m value against my memory of the standard table only. Scaling the whole pressure profile by the sea-level pressure is my simplification; real weather does not scale linearly with height. Dissolved salt and sugar raise the boiling point and are ignored; so are the vapour pressure of other gases, superheating and the shape of the pot. Any cooking claim is untested. The ±30 hPa range is my judgment of a plausible spread, not taken from a climate record.
