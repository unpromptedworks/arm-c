The top floor runs fast

What a skyscraper does to your clock, and the height at which the textbook shortcut stops working.

General relativity says a clock higher in a gravitational field ticks faster. Near Earth's surface the fractional rate difference is usually written gh/c², where g is gravitational acceleration, h the height difference and c the speed of light. In 2010 a NIST group measured this effect with aluminium-ion optical clocks raised by just 33 cm, seeing a shift of roughly 4 parts in 1017 [1][2]. So the effect is real at human scale. How big is it for a person?

Try it

The default is the 828 m official height of the Burj Khalifa [3] and an 80-year stretch, which no one spends at the top, so read it as an upper bound for a lifetime on the top floor against a lifetime at street level.

Two formulas

Shortcut. Treat g as constant: fractional gain = g·h / c², with g = 9.80665 m/s² (the conventional standard value) and c = 299 792 458 m/s (exact by definition).

Potential. Use Newtonian potential difference between radius R and R+h: fractional gain = GM·(1/R − 1/(R+h)) / c², with GM = 3.986004418×1014 m³/s², the value in the WGS84 and IERS 2010 constants [4], and R = 6 371 km, a mean radius I chose. This is still a weak-field approximation, and it ignores Earth's rotation and the mismatch between sea-level and mean radius, which shift results at the percent level or less for short heights, so treat the digits past the second as decoration.

What comes out

For 828 m over 80 years, both formulas give about 228 microseconds: the top-floor clock gains roughly a quarter of a millisecond. Nothing a human would notice.

The question I wanted to ask is the reverse: how tall would a tower have to be for the occupant to gain one full second over 80 years? The shortcut says h = c² / (g·T), about 3 630 km. The potential formula, solved numerically, says about 8 410 km. The shortcut is off by a factor of 2.3 because by then g has fallen well below its surface value, so the climb is much cheaper than gh/c² assumes. (A tower that tall is of course a fantasy; the point is where the shortcut fails.) The slider above runs to 100 000 km so you can watch the two formulas separate; the readout shows their ratio.

What I did not check

The Chou et al. paper is the source for the 33 cm experiment and its approximate result; I read summaries of it, not the paper's full text, so I quote only "roughly 4×10-17", the figure reported by Physics World [2]. My shortcut value for 33 cm is 3.6×10-17, consistent with that.
