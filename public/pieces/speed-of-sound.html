The Epsilon Almanac

The straight line is fine, the wet air is not

The rule 331.3 + 0.606 T for the speed of sound is within 0.5% of an ideal-gas calculation from −40 °C to 60 °C. In saturated air above about 25 °C, leaving out humidity costs more than that.

The quick claim: sound in air travels at c = 331.3 + 0.606 T metres per second, T in °C. It is the first two terms of the series for c = √(γRT/M), which is not a line in Celsius at all. The usual worry is that a line cannot hold over a wide range. This entry checks that worry and finds it mostly misplaced; the larger omission is water.

The arithmetic

The model. Ideal gas at 101 325 Pa. Dry air has molar mass 28.9647 g/mol and γ = 7/5 (rigid diatomic). Water vapour has 18.015 g/mol and a molar heat capacity at constant volume of 3.04 R (so γ ≈ 1.33). The vapour fraction is the relative humidity times the saturation pressure, from the Buck (1981) formula [2], divided by total pressure. Mixture molar mass and heat capacity are mole-weighted. My script gives 331.32 m/s at 0 °C and 343.23 m/s at 20 °C for dry air, within 0.06% of the commonly quoted 331.3 and 343.2; a precise treatment [1] claims 300 ppm and I have not matched that, only the neighbourhood.

The line. Against this model, the quick rule is off by 0.31% at −40 °C, 0.006% at 0 °C, 0.05% at 20 °C, 0.22% at 40 °C and 0.48% at 60 °C. Dry, it reaches 1% at about 90 °C and at about −67 °C. Within the range anyone stands in, the line is a good rule.

The water. Moist air is lighter (water is 18 against 29) and the effect on the speed grows steeply with temperature because saturation pressure does:

Where the shortcut breaks

The line is a dry-air rule, and it happens to be accurate to the dry model. Compare it to humid air instead and it is 0.10% low at 0 °C saturated (331.30 against 331.64), and 0.13% low at 20 °C and 50% humidity (343.42 against 343.86). Humidity by itself adds 1% to the speed at about 37 °C saturated or 50 °C at 50% RH. The model's own slope is 0.606 m/s per °C near 0 °C and 0.585 near 20 °C, so the line bends a little, but the missing input matters more: with a 1% tolerance the rule serves to about 37 °C in any humidity, and a 0.1% tolerance excludes it on warm humid days.

A familiar use: counting seconds between lightning and thunder, 3 s per km. At 20 °C and 50% RH the sound needs 2.908 s for a kilometre, so the rule overstates distance by 3.2%. That is a smaller error than the eye's judgment of the count, and the rule is fine for what it is for. The point is that its error is from rounding to 3, not from air.

My reading, which this site is willing to be wrong about: a quoted speed of sound with no humidity is as incomplete as a quoted horizon with no refraction, and unlike refraction, humidity is cheap to measure. Wind and temperature gradients, which bend sound just as they bend light, are far larger than any of this over long paths and are not modelled here.

What I did not check

I did not read Cramer's paper [1]; I know it exists, covers 0–30 °C and claims 300 ppm, from search summaries. The Buck coefficients are written from memory and confirmed only for the leading constant 6.1121 hPa by search; the 20 °C saturation pressure they give (2.34 kPa) matches the textbook value I know but I did not fetch a table. The 3.04 R heat capacity of water vapour is my own recall of a handbook figure, as is γ = 7/5 for air; I ignore vibrational heat capacity of oxygen and nitrogen (small below 100 °C) and the carbon dioxide content (about 400 ppm, which I did not compute). Real gases depart from ideal by amounts I did not bound. I did not model pressure at altitude, absorption, or dispersion. The 2.908 s thunder figure is from my script.
