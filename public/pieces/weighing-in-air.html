The scale reads the water low

A kilogram of water on a balance adjusted with steel weights reads about a tenth of a percent light, in any weather. The weather moves that by a fifth of itself, and the humidity by a hundredth.

The quick claim: a balance shows the mass of what is on it. It does not. It balances a force, and the air pushes up on the load by the weight of the air the load displaces. The balance was adjusted with weights of a different volume, so the two pushes differ. The correction depends on the body's density, the weights' density and the air's density, and the third is the one that changes from day to day.

Try it

The density slider is logarithmic and runs from 100 to about 15 800 kg/m³; water (about 1 000) sits at three-tenths of the way along. Pressure and temperature are held to the range the CIPM formula is recommended for (600 to 1 100 hPa, 15 to 27 °C) [1].

The arithmetic

The convention. OIML R 33 defines the "conventional value of the result of weighing in air" as the mass of a standard of density 8 000 kg/m³ that balances the body in air of density 1.2 kg/m³ at 20 °C. Its Annex A.1 writes the balance condition as m(1 − 1.2/ρ) = m′(1 − 1.2/8000) and the result m′ = m[1 − 1.2(1/ρ − 1/8000)] [2]. That is the whole correction. For water (ρ ≈ 998 kg/m³) it is −1 052 ppm, so 1 kg of water has a conventional value of 998.95 g. I get the same number from the exact ratio in the tool, which does not drop the second-order term; they differ by less than 1 ppm.

The air. The convention fixes the air at 1.2 kg/m³. The CIPM-2007 equation gives the real value: ρa = (p·Ma / ZRT)·(1 − xv(1 − Mv/Ma)), with Ma = 28.965 46 g/mol, R = 8.314 472, xv the water-vapour mole fraction from relative humidity, and Z a compressibility factor Z within a few hundredths of a percent of 1 [1]. I implemented the equations as printed in the paper's appendix A and checked them against two things. Dry air at 15 °C and 101 325 Pa comes out at 1.2255 kg/m³, the usual standard-atmosphere 1.225. At 20 °C, 50 % humidity and 101 325 Pa it is 1.1993, so the convention's 1.2 is a sea-level, 20 °C, half-humid room, to four digits.

Where the shortcut breaks

"The scale reads the mass" is off by 1% for anything lighter than about 118 kg/m³ and by 0.1% for anything lighter than about 1 043 kg/m³, which includes water and everything made mostly of it. In the other direction, the 0.1% error is not a mystery of the weather: moving the same water from sea level to 840 hPa (about 1 500 m up in a standard atmosphere, my calculation, not looked up) changes it by 207 ppm, a fifth of the effect. Humidity is smaller again. For water at 20 °C and 101 325 Pa, going from dry air to saturated air changes the reading by 10.5 ppm. So the ranking, largest to smallest for water, is: the body's density, the pressure, the temperature (about 5 ppm per kelvin, so roughly 60 ppm over the permitted 12 °C), and the humidity.

My reading, which this site is willing to be wrong about: for weighing water, a barometer earns its place before a hygrometer. The full humidity swing is 10 ppm of the reading, which a pressure change of about 10 hPa also produces, and ordinary weather moves the pressure by more than that (not looked up). The CIPM paper's sensitivities say the same in air-density terms: about +1×10⁻⁵ per pascal of pressure error, so 1 hPa is 1 000 ppm of the air density; about −9×10⁻³ per unit of relative humidity, so a 10% error is 900 ppm; and about −4×10⁻³ per kelvin [1].

The air-density formula itself is not the limit: the paper gives a combined standard uncertainty of 22 parts per million of the air density, so about 0.00003 kg/m³ at 1.2, which on a water weighing is far below 1 ppm [1]. The assumed CO₂ fraction (400 µmol/mol) moves the air density by 0.4 × the CO₂ error [1], again irrelevant here.

A shortcut for the air: ideal-gas dry air at 20 °C and 101 325 Pa is 1.2041 kg/m³ against the CIPM 1.2046, a difference of 0.04%, about 0.5 ppm on water, so the ideal-gas law is a safe shortcut for the air and not for the load.

What I did not check

I read the CIPM-2007 paper and OIML R 33 (1979 edition) in full where cited; I did not read the current OIML R 111, which supersedes R 33 for weights, so the 8 000 kg/m³ and 1.2 kg/m³ values are R 33's. I did not check that every constant in my code matches the paper beyond the two spot checks above and the paper's own statement that its Appendix A constants are unchanged from CIPM-81/91. I assumed the balance's sensitivity does not itself depend on the air, that the body's density is exact and the same at all temperatures, and that the weights used to adjust it have density exactly 8 000 kg/m³ (many internal weights do not). The 207 ppm and 10.5 ppm figures are from my own code. Real balances also differ by the local gravity and by drift; neither is modelled. The densities in the table are from memory.
