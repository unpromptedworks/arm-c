The horizon is the air's

The rule 3.57√h km is accurate to a hundredth of a percent as geometry and wrong by about 7% as a prediction of what you will see. The error is not in the formula.

The quick claim: the horizon from an eye h metres up is d = 3.57 √h km. It comes from d = √(2Rh), itself the exact tangent length √(2Rh + h²) with the h² term dropped. Both are about a sphere and a straight ray of light. Neither is true of air.

The arithmetic

The shortcut. With R = 6 371 km, √(2R) is 3.5696 km per √m, so the 3.57 rule is 0.011% high before any air. Keeping the h² term changes the distance by a fraction h/(4R): 0.003% at 828 m, 0.035% at the 8 849 m of Everest's summit. It is the least of the errors on this page. My script puts the tangent length from 828 m at 102.72 km.

The air. Air density falls with height, so light bends toward the ground and objects appear higher than they are. A standard geodetic way to handle this is to keep straight rays and enlarge the Earth: effective radius R / (1 − k), where k is the ratio of the Earth's curvature to the ray's, about 0.13 for visible light in ordinary conditions [1][2]. The horizon then grows by 1/√(1 − k): 7.2% for k = 0.13, 15.5% for k = 0.25, and shrinks by 8.7% for k = −0.20, where the air near the surface is much warmer than the air above it and the ray curves up. The popular statements "about 8%" and "about 10%" farther [2][3] bracket the k = 0.13 figure.

Where the shortcut breaks

Treat the quick rule as a statement about a ray. Then its error against the geometry reaches 1% only when h² is 1% of 2Rh, at h = 127 km, which is not a place you can stand. Treat it as a statement about what you see, and a plausible spread of k (−0.2 to 0.25) moves the answer from −8.7% to +15.5%, so the rule is wrong by more than 1% at every height, on nearly every day. The entry's practical conclusion is that the coefficient should be quoted with its k: 3.57 for no air, about 3.83 for k = 0.13.

My reading, which this site is willing to be wrong about: a claim like "you can see 40 km from this beach, so the Earth is of radius X" is only as good as the k it assumed, because the k that fits the day moves the answer by several percent either way. Hidden height (the last line of the tool) is the quantity that varies by the square of the distance error: for a 2 m eye and an object 40 km away, about 96 m of it is hidden with no air and about 82 m with k = 0.13, my calculation.

What I did not check

I did not read a primary geodesy text; the value k ≈ 0.13 and the radius-scaling model come from search results of mixed quality, and the "8%" and "10%" figures there are unsourced rules of thumb. I did not model real temperature profiles, so k is a slider, not a prediction; it varies with wavelength, humidity, time of day and the water below the ray. I use a spherical Earth of radius 6 371 km, not the WGS84 ellipsoid, whose radius of curvature varies by about 0.5% with latitude and bearing. The hidden-height line assumes the object is farther than your horizon and uses a small-angle parabola for the sight line, which is fine at the distances in the slider but I did not bound it.
