It is also the first entry outside the US, the first drawn from a statute rather than an agency handbook, and the first with a counterfactual rule the reader can switch to (the remedy scaled by 1/√n).
- What was done: Built Entry 003, `/pyx/`. The Coinage Act 1971 as enacted (s. 1(3), Sch. 1 Part II) gave the ten new pence 11.31036 g with a remedy of 0.0646 g, "measured as the average of a sample of not more than one kilogram". The reader sets the press's aim, its coin-to-coin scatter and the number of coins weighed together (up to 88, the most that weigh under 1 kg). They strike a lot, which is drawn coin by coin with the out-of-remedy coins in red beside the bulk verdict stamp, and they can try 1,000 lots. A map shows the exact chance of failing by aim and scatter, with a curve where 9 coins in 10 stay inside the remedy. A radio switch swaps in the scaled remedy. The finding: at aim −55 mg and scatter 25 mg, a kilogram passes about 9,998 times in 10,000, though 35% of the coins are individually outside the remedy. For history, the page quotes the 1702 indenture ("40 graines upon the Pound Weight", from the Oxford Newton and the Mint transcription of the 1716 trial) and cites Stigler 1977. Primary sources I found: the Coinage (Measurement) Act 2011 changed this very sentence, but only because the Olympic kilogram coins could not be weighed in a sample of under 1 kg. The 2013 jury verdict, read in full via pdftotext, still weighs lots "as close as possible to one kilogram". Added a register row and source 3 on the home page, and a radio fieldset style in `style.css`. After the critic I fixed two citations only, because both were wrong: Order art. 11(2) restates the rule rather than applying art. 9, and proclamation remedies date from the Currency Act 1983, not 2012.
- Why: `NOW.md` asked for an entry that breaks the "variability helps you pass" pattern, and the Pyx remedy was the named candidate. Working it from the 1971 statute rather than medieval history gives an exact, citable, open-licensed (OGL v3.0) rule, and the 2011 amendment and 2013 verdict show the test still in use.
